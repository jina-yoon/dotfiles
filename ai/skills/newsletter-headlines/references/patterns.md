# Title formats that have worked, with real examples

The same archive as `archive.md`, sliced by format. Use this when you want to generate options in a specific register, or to check whether a format is getting overused. Formats aren't a template to fill in mechanically — they're proof that a certain *kind of promise* lands with this audience. The promise is what matters; the wording should follow the piece.

Length note: Gmail on mobile truncates around 35-40 characters. Titles longer than that still get published (some of the best performers are longer), but everything load-bearing needs to be in the first ~40 chars. "LLMs are picking winners. Here's how to become one" works truncated because "LLMs are picking winners" is a complete, provocative claim on its own.

---

## 1. What nobody tells you / what people get wrong

Promises insider knowledge and implies the reader currently has a wrong model. The strongest and most-used format in the archive.

- What nobody tells you about writing agent skills
- What nobody tells developers about documentation
- The stuff nobody tells you about startup marketing
- What engineers get wrong about communication
- What engineers get wrong about analytics
- Job interview questions engineers should ask (but don't)
- Non-obvious pricing advice for startups
- Non-obvious SEO advice for startups
- Non-obvious behaviors that will kill your startup

Works because: names a specific gap between the reader's model and reality. Fails when the topic is one where nobody would expect hidden knowledge in the first place.

## 2. Mistakes to avoid / you're doing it wrong

Blunt, accusatory, high open rates. Reader recognizes themselves in the accusation.

- Avoid these AI coding mistakes
- Don't make these feature flag mistakes
- A/B testing mistakes I learned the hard way
- You're doing quarterly planning wrong
- This is why you're not shipping
- Why you're bad at giving feedback
- Your product ideas probably suck (that's ok)
- How first-time founders fail
- Failure modes for engineering team leads
- 6 mistakes we've made at PostHog (and what we learned)
- Stop being the code review bottleneck

Works because: loss aversion. Only use when the piece actually delivers the mistakes — an accusatory title over a gentle how-to feels like a bait and switch.

## 3. WTF is X (and why should you care?)

Explainer framing that gives permission not to already know. Reserved for genuinely jargon-y or contested terms.

- WTF is activation and why should engineers care?
- WTF does a product manager do? (and why engineers should care)
- Why we're bullish on loops (subtitle: WTF are loops, why is everyone arguing about them...)

Works because: the reader has seen the term and is quietly unsure. Fails on a term everyone already understands.

## 4. How to think like X / an engineer's guide to Y

Positions the reader as borrowing a skill from an adjacent discipline.

- How to think like a growth engineer
- An engineer's guide to talking to users
- An engineer's guide to vibe design (with prompts)
- Pragmatic startup lessons from Elon
- Skills for developers from the product manager playbook (as a subtitle)

Works because: role transfer. Best when the source role is clearly *not* the reader's.

## 5. The magic of / the hidden X

Contrarian reveal — something ordinary turns out to be powerful, or something good turns out to have a cost.

- The magic of small engineering teams
- The hidden danger of shipping fast
- The hidden benefits of being an open-source startup
- Great companies are built in hackathons
- Doing support makes you a better engineer
- Using your own product is a superpower ← flagged internally as too generic; the noun ("your own product") isn't specific enough to create curiosity

Works because: reframes something the reader already has an opinion about. Fails when the "hidden" thing is obvious.

## 6. What we learned / N things we've learned

House format. The credibility comes from the specificity of the experience, and it belongs in the title or subtitle.

- 32 things we've learned about building a startup that scales
- 43 things we've learned about hiring at PostHog
- 50 things we've learned about building successful products
- 40 things we've learned about marketing for developers
- 24 tips for giving S-tier demos
- 10 things we've learned about A/B testing for startups
- What we wish we knew about building AI agents
- What we learned about hiring from our first five employees
- What we've learned about building AI-powered features
- Everything we've learned about hiring for startups (so far)

Works because: earned experience, at a countable scale. Use a real number tied to a real body of work — an arbitrary number reads as listicle filler. This format has run roughly annually; check `archive.md` before reaching for it again.

## 7. Flat declarative / provocation

Very short, opinionated, no hedging. Highest ceiling and highest risk.

- Collaboration sucks
- Product management is broken. Engineers can fix it
- How startups lose their edge
- The engineeringification of everything
- 2030-shaped software
- Good taste makes great products
- Beyond the 10x engineer
- 10x job posts for 10x engineers
- Glue teams vs back-office teams
- LLMs are picking winners. Here's how to become one
- How not to be boring
- The deadline doom loop

Works because: it's a position, not a topic. This is the format most worth trying even when another format seems safer — it survives truncation, and it forces you to say what you actually think.

## 8. Process / behind the scenes

We-did-a-thing, here's how. Needs a number or a named result to earn attention.

- We used context engineering to 5x conversion and 2x activation
- How we got our first 1,000 users
- How we choose technologies
- How we decide what to build
- How we work asynchronously
- How bolt.new works
- Defining our ICP is the most important thing we ever did

Works because: proof. A concrete outcome in the title does most of the work.

# Subtitle patterns

The subtitle's job is to resolve just enough of the title's tension to make opening feel worthwhile, without giving away the piece. If the title is a provocation, the subtitle should say what the reader gets. If the title is descriptive, the subtitle should add the voice.

**Say plainly what the reader gets** (pairs with a provocative title):

- "A simple guide to agent autonomy" (title: How much can you delegate to agents?)
- "A startup's guide to answer engine optimization" (title: LLMs are picking winners. Here's how to become one)
- "4 ways to make AI code review suck less (with prompts)" (title: Stop being the code review bottleneck)
- "Red flags for teams that want to ship fast" (title: This is why you're not shipping)
- "Five principles to develop your product intuition for agents"

**Add the credibility / receipts**:

- "What growing PostHog from 11 to 150 people taught us about culture, hiring, product, marketing, and more"
- "In the last 12 months, we've received 30,000 applications, interviewed 2,000 candidates, and hired 65 people. We learned some stuff."
- "Lessons learned from two years of building AI agents at PostHog"
- "Lessons from GitLab, Doist, PostHog, and more"

**Be funny / self-aware** (pairs with a plain title):

- "Win the web with 10 weird tricks, Google hates me"
- "How to NOT lose a billion dollars (or your job)"
- "Ironic coming from the 'collaboration sucks' guy"
- "Money, fame, pride... hedgehogs?"
- "Product + hedgehog cartoons = IPO?"
- "Stand out or die trying"
- "Tips for breaking into the 0.4%"
- "Doing weird stuff on the internet is optional but recommended"

**The trailing ellipsis** — subtitle completes the title's sentence:

- "...and how to avoid them." (title: Failure modes for engineering team leads)
- "... and how to escape it!" (title: The deadline doom loop)
- "...or how to join the right startup and stand out from the crowd"

**Short and blunt**:

- "Don't get this wrong"
- "Don't make our mistakes"
- "Bad experiments are worse than none at all"
- "Early hires will define your culture"
- "Great tech + AI = profit $"

# Rough engagement signal

Raw like counts aren't comparable across years — the list grew from ~5k to 50k+ subscribers — but within their era these stood out:

- **2024**: Product management is broken. Engineers can fix it (230). Beyond the 10x engineer (73).
- **2025**: Collaboration sucks (176). 50 things we've learned about building successful products (156). An engineer's guide to vibe design, with prompts (127). Job interview questions engineers should ask, but don't (113). What engineers get wrong about communication (109).
- **2026**: The golden rules of agent-first product engineering (93). The hidden danger of shipping fast (95). Stop being the code review bottleneck (88). WTF does a product manager do? (85).

The pattern across all three years: **strong opinions and named enemies beat neutral topic descriptions**. "Collaboration sucks" and "Product management is broken" are the two biggest hits in the archive, and neither describes what the piece is about — they stake out a position. The lowest performers are the ones that describe a topic without taking a side.
